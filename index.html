Research paper

Newton's Cooling Law Fitted to One Coffee Curve Does Not Transfer Between Cups

Newton's law of cooling is the standard model for hot drinks, but its accuracy on real cups is rarely measured. We fitted it, and a one-term power-law extension, to the first 30 minutes of one measured coffee curve, froze the constants, and tested them on seven published curves. The frozen Newton-law constant missed by 8.3 K on average (95% interval 6.5 to 9.9 K) against a target of 0.5 K. The 30-minute figure describes the cup the constant came from, not cups in general.

Contributions

  • A power-law extension with a small, unestablished gain

    Over seven held-out curves, its mean 30-minute RMS error is 0.19 K lower than Newton's law (paired 95% interval −0.44 to −0.00 K). A resampling test gives p = 0.0625, so the difference is not established.

  • A frozen constant misses by about 8 K

    With its constant frozen, Newton's law has a mean error of 8.3 K over the seven curves. The registered target was under 0.5 K, so the frozen test failed.

  • An exponent of 2.18 on one cup

    Fitted to the coffee curve, the in-window exponent is 2.18 (profile 95% interval 1.95 to 2.42). It was not tested in advance and does not carry across cups without refitting.

  • Refitting to early data predicts the Langer cups well

    Refitting the constant to each curve's first 10 minutes predicts the rest with a mean RMS error of 0.64 K over eleven curves. The coffee cup stays at 5.3 K. This was not tested in advance.

Method

The question is how far a Newton-law constant fitted to one cup predicts the 30-minute temperature of other cups. The test has three steps, and each one changes what the number means.

  1. In. Eight cooling curves from three sources. The fitting curve is a coffee curve measured in a porcelain pot, 29 points from 0 to 138.5 min, read from the vector data of the source figure. The held-out set holds a Manitoba coffee cup on a laboratory table and six 237 mL cups of hot water from Langer et al. (2018), read from figures. The fitting curve is Code: Coffee cooling curve from one published pot study; the held-out curves are Code: Published hot-liquid cooling curves for held-out tests.
  2. Process. Both laws are fitted by least squares to the first 30 minutes of the fitting curve, with 20 random starts. The constants are then frozen. Newton's law gives T(t) = Ta + (T0 − Ta) exp(−kt). The power-law extension sets dT/dt = −kc (T − Ta)x, and Newton's law is the case x = 1. Ambient temperature is 20 °C where none is reported.
  3. Out. Each held-out curve gets an RMS error over 0 < t ≤ 30 min, in kelvin, and the mean across curves is the headline figure. The target was fixed before any held-out curve was evaluated.

The frozen test is strict on purpose. A model that fits its own curve well can still miss a second cup, and the frozen test is the check that catches this. Intervals are bootstrap intervals over 1000 resamples. Data sources and reading errors are listed under the limitations below.

Refitting to the first 10 minutes

When each held-out curve's constant is refitted to its first 10 minutes and tested over minutes 10 to 30 (Figure 3), the mean RMS error over eleven curves is 0.64 K for Newton's law and 0.60 K for the power law at x = 2.18. The mean is carried by the Manitoba coffee cup, where Newton's law has 5.30 K and the power law 0.77 K. On the ten Langer cups, Newton's law has a mean of 0.18 K, and every cup lies below 0.3 K.

Limitations

  • Scope of the evidence. The fitting curve is one measured coffee curve in a porcelain pot. The dataset has eight curves from three sources, and seven of them are held out from fitting. Six of the eight are hot-water cups from one lab and protocol, so they are not six independent measurements. Only one held-out curve is coffee.
  • Missing variables. No source reports relative humidity or a lid or surface cover. Evaporation and the effect of a lid are therefore not tested. A literature check that found no accessible source recording humidity with a cooling curve is Code: Newton's law vs published coffee-cup cooling data. It covers only what could be read.
  • Ambient temperature. The ambient temperature was not reported for the fitting curve or for the Langer cups, and 20 °C is an assumption. Moving from ambient A to ambient B changes the Manitoba error by 0.6 K for Newton's law and by 0.8 K for the power law. The cause of this shift is not isolated.
  • Parameter bounds. Four of the ten Langer refits reach the −10 °C lower bound of the ambient search, and two more fitted ambient temperatures are outside a plausible range for a room. These refits reduce confidence in the refit errors for those cups.
  • Agreement with published values. Our reading of the fitting curve did not reproduce the fit statistics the source reports. The 1.26 K value is used throughout, and the cause is open.
  • Reading from figures. The Langer curves and the fitting curve were read from figures. Reading errors are small compared with the 8 K main result but not compared with the 0.3 K refit errors. The Langer crossing times differ from the source's stated values, so no crossing time is used.
  • Sensitivity. Four further Langer curves were read but kept out of the planned set. Evaluating all ten with the frozen parameters gives 9.17 K for Newton's law and 9.05 K for the power law, so the planned set does not drive the result. The eight-curve dataset that underlies the held-out set is Code: Published hot-liquid cooling curves for held-out tests.
  • Reference records. The journal of the fitting-curve study and the Crossref record of the Langer et al. study could not be verified. They are cited by author and year only.
