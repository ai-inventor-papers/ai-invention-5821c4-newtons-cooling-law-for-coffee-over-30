How accurately Newton's cooling law predicts a cup of coffee over 30 minutes

The run asks how accurately Newton's law of cooling predicts the temperature of an open cup of coffee over 30 minutes, compared with published measurements. In sample, on one published porcelain pot coffee curve, the constant-rate law has a 30-minute RMS of 1.26 K with room temperature fixed at 20 C (1.12 K with room temperature fitted at a bound), and a one-term nonlinear convection law fits the same window to 0.33 K with exponent 2.18. The exponent is not stable across fits, which range from 1.20 to 2.18. Held out, the porcelain pot parameters transfer poorly: on the Manitoba coffee curve the constant-rate law is off by 3.45 K RMS and the nonlinear law by 2.66 K, and on six Langer hot-water curves both laws is off by by about 9 K. The 0.5 K accuracy target is not met under strict transfer. Humidity, covering layer-layer and geometry terms remain not tested because no available series records them. The question is in part answered.

The run tests how accurately Newton's law of cooling predicts a 30-minute open-cup coffee curve against published data. The current finding has three parts. In sample, the constant-rate law is off the first 30 minutes of one porcelain pot coffee curve by 1.26 K RMS with room temperature at 20 C, and the figure depends on that assumption. A one-term nonlinear law reduces the in-sample RMS to 0.33 K, but its exponent (1.20 to 2.18 across fits) is not stable. Out of sample, the constant-rate law is off by 3.45 K RMS on the held-out Manitoba coffee curve and the nonlinear law by 2.66 K, and six Langer hot-water curves by about 9 K for both laws; the 0.5 K target is not met, and the nonlinear law's mean advantage over seven held-out curves (0.19 K) is not significant under a paired permutation test. Humidity, covering layer-layer and geometry effects are not tested.
